\section{Introduction}\label{sec:experiments_introduction}

At last, this chapter summarizes the experiments carried out on the framework described in the previous Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}.

% This chapter reports the experimental campaign carried out over the simulation framework of Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}. Experiment 1 validates the simulated machine against the published Case 1 study it derives from, establishing the dynamic behavior of the plant without fine stages. Experiment 2 trains and evaluates the convolutional network on the WM-811K dataset, establishing the classifier half of the pipeline. Experiment 3 connects the two: it verifies that disturbances injected into the simulation produce wafer-map signatures the CNN can react to, and that sweeping the kinematic bounds $(v_{\max}, a_{\max}, j_{\max}, s_{\max})$ shifts the exposure error --- and therefore the defect map --- reproducibly. Experiment 4 evaluates the control configurations of Section~\ref{sec:methodology_control}: lag-compensation feedforward, fine-stage PI, and the nonlinear HIGS loop, quantifying the contribution of each. Experiment 5 is the central demonstration of the thesis premise: as the settling budget shrinks, trajectory-induced failures emerge as a spatially structured, scanner-plausible pattern, and the yield-aware cost traces the throughput--yield transition. Experiment 6 runs the optimization scenarios E3 and E4, comparing the conventional servo cost against the CNN cost inside the same particle-swarm search over the exact snap-bounded profile family. Experiment 7 crosses profile order with controller quality, quantifying whether trajectory smoothness matters when the controller is poor and how much it is worth when the controller is good. All results reported below are outputs of the actual scripts and trained models of this project; where a result falls short of the criterion set in Chapter~\ref{ch:control_objectives}, this is stated and analyzed rather than omitted.

\section{Experiment 1: Reproduction of the Case 1 Study}\label{sec:exp_case1}

The first experiment reproduces Case 1 of the reference study by Al-Rawashdeh et al.~\cite{Al-Rawashdeh2022_Caracterization}. Following the in-plane ($x, y, \theta_z$) planar formulation established in Chapter~\ref{ch:model}, step-and-scan motion of the wafer and reticle chains is evaluated without the short-stroke stages. Under this baseline configuration, motion is governed solely by the long-stroke stage using the collocated PD controller of Section~\ref{sec:methodology_control}; the integral component is omitted to prevent windup during high-acceleration ramps. We aim to verify that the simulation reproduces the qualitative dynamic behavior reported in the literature, and to quantify how far a long-stroke-only machine falls short of the exposure specifications.

The step-and-scan sequence is performed over four dies ($32 \times 32$\,mm$^2$ each in a single row) with baseline trajectory parameters $v_{w,scan} = 0.8$\,m/s, $a_{w,\max} = 20$\,m/s$^2$, $j_{\max} = 1600$\,m/s$^3$, and $s_{\max} = 10^5$\,m/s$^4$. The scan profile lasts $160.8$\,ms, with the first exposure window falling at $[53.6, 107.2]$\,ms into the run, and the complete hold-plus-four-die sequence spans $1.843$\,s. The reticle moves at $3.2$\,m/s with $a_{r,\max} = 80$\,m/s$^2$ in the opposite direction. \section{Experiment 3: Trajectory-to-Defect Pipeline}\label{sec:exp_pipeline}

The third experiment evaluates the full traversal of the 300\,mm wafer, bridging the physical plant of Experiment 1 with the classifier of Experiment 2. Under long-stroke tracking only, two directions are explored: disturbance injection under fixed trajectory parameters, and a sweep of the kinematic bounds ($v_{w,scan}$, $a_{w,\max}$, $j_{\max}$, $s_{\max}$) under zero disturbance.

With an unactuated short-stroke stage, the exposure-window MSD of the synchronization error lies between 40 and 272\,\textmu m depending on the trajectory, nearly four orders of magnitude above the 7\,nm specification. Consequently, applying an absolute threshold marks every die as failing, yielding saturated and uninformative wafer maps.

Therefore, for the studies conducted under Case 1 control, the evaluation criterion is the \textit{relative} effect of the disturbance or trajectory parameters, referenced to the unperturbed baseline.

\subsection{Disturbance signatures}\label{sec:exp_perturb}

Three disturbance types are injected as forces on the wafer-stage end-effector: broadband force noise on both axes (floor \textit{vibration}), a sustained bias force during scans near the wafer edge (\textit{drift}), and a sustained bias force across a single die column (\textit{scratch}). Synchronization errors are evaluated during the constant-velocity exposure window. A die is marked as failing when its MSD exceeds twice the baseline median or when its mean error shifts by more than 100\,nm relative to the unperturbed run.

This campaign uses a fine grid (241 dies of $16\times16$\,mm$^2$) so that line- and point-like patterns retain their morphology after resizing to the CNN input. Table~\ref{tab:exp3_perturb} and Figure~\ref{fig:exp3_perturb_maps} present the results.

The spatial component functions as intended since the unperturbed run yields a clean wafer, and each disturbance produces a distinct failure pattern ---scattered dies under force noise, an outer ring under edge bias, and a 15-die column under column bias. Nonetheless, the classification performance aligns only partially with the target classes. \subsection{Kinematic-bound sweep}\label{sec:exp_sweep}

The second campaign sweeps the kinematic bounds around the baseline ($0.8\,\mathrm{m/s}$, $20\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$, $1600\,\mathrm{m/s^3}$, $10^5\,\mathrm{m/s^4}$) while holding disturbances at zero on a coarser grid (49 dies of $32\times32$\,mm$^2$). An aggressive configuration raises all four bounds, a conservative configuration relaxes all four, and four intermediate configurations relax one bound at a time.

Table~\ref{tab:exp3_sweep} summarizes the seven configurations. The median exposure MSD responds monotonically to all four kinematic bounds. Relative to the baseline (101.8\,\textmu m MSD, 24 of 49 dies failing), the aggressive configuration causes the entire wafer to fail, driving the median MSD nearly threefold to 272.3\,\textmu m. Conversely, the conservative configuration reduces the median MSD by more than half (to 40.0\,\textmu m) with only a single failing die. Relaxing any single bound progressively lowers the median error---from 90.2\,\textmu m when halving acceleration down to 56.7\,\textmu m with a tighter snap---while extending the scan profile to 248\,ms compared to 161\,ms at baseline. This live snap response is possible because the exact profile generator (Section~\ref{sec:methodology_trajectories}) enforces $s_{\max}$ directly rather than deriving it implicitly from ramp duration.

\section{Experiment 4: Evaluation of Control Configurations}\label{sec:exp_controllers}

The pipeline experiments above ran under Case 1 control, where the exposure error is millimetre-scale and the failure thresholds had to be scaled accordingly. This experiment adds, one at a time, the mechanisms of Section~\ref{sec:methodology_control} that make fourth-order trajectories trackable, and measures the contribution of each on the baseline trajectory over a four-die row in the full simulation model. Table~\ref{tab:exp4_controllers} reports the cruise-window indices of the offset-corrected synchronization error; Figure~\ref{fig:exp4_controllers} shows the error traces.

Experiments prior to this section ran under long-stroke-only control (\texttt{Case 1}), where the MSD shoots up into the milimeter range. This section progressively builds up the components of Section~\ref{sec:methodology_control}, allowing us to measure the contribution of each over the original four-die row, and furthermore, to make fourth-order trajectories trackable. Three observations follow. First, the identified lag feedforward reduces the exposure-window MA by nearly an order of magnitude (955.0 to 99.8\,\textmu m): on the frictionless plant the dominant Case 1 error is the acceleration-proportional lag of the chain --- servo deflection plus coupling stretch, largest on the reticle chain at $80$\,m/s$^2$ --- and its quasi-static part is cancelled open-loop by commanding the long stroke ahead of the reference (Eq.~\eqref{eq:lag_ff}); what survives the fit is the \textit{dynamic} part, the decaying ringing of the flexible couplings after each ramp, which no quasi-static feedforward can represent. Second, the fine loops attack exactly that transient and reduce the exposure MSD by a further factor of $\approx 7.8\times$ (6.54\,\textmu m to 0.834--0.851\,\textmu m), a combined improvement of $\approx 128\times$ on MA and $\approx 30\times$ on MSD over Case 1; the residual MA of 7.48--7.65\,\textmu m is the tail of the post-ramp transient still decaying at cruise entry, and it is bounded by the fine loop's authority --- the short-stroke actuator's own velocity loop places its command-following pole at $K_P/K_V = 100$\,rad/s, and the passive Stage element's 180\,Hz resonance sets the stability boundary at $k_p \approx 16$ against the flexible-chain modes. Third, at their respective stability-boundary tunings the linear PI and the nonlinear HIGS loop land within a few percent of each other, with \textbf{HIGS marginally ahead on both indices} at this cruise metric (7.48/0.834\,\textmu m vs.\ 7.65/0.851\,\textmu m for MA/MSD, HIGS better by $\approx 2\,\%$): HIGS sustains an integrator frequency roughly $42\times$ the PI's equivalent $k_i/k_p$ before destabilizing, expressing its phase advantage as usable gain. The regime where the HIGS advantage becomes decisive, overshoot-constrained settling, is not isolated by the present cruise-tracking metric. A word on the residual scale is in order. The reference study reports that the same two-stage architecture --- without any fine stage --- already reaches an exposure MSD in the tens of nanometres (Fig.~14 of~\cite{Al-Rawashdeh2022_Caracterization}), within a small factor of the $7$\,nm bound, which the optional fine stage then closes. This replication, still two-stage, settles roughly two orders of magnitude coarser (Case 3b cruise MSD $\approx 0.834$\,\textmu m, MA $\approx 7.48$\,\textmu m). The gap is a property of the servo model, and specifically of its \textit{bandwidth} rather than its stiffness. The present model replaces the reference machine's essentially rigid actuator mounts ($10^{12}$\,N/m in the published parameter set) with a generic position servo ($K_P = 10^7$\,N/m) whose command-following authority is capped at its velocity-loop pole, $K_P/K_V = 100$\,rad/s ($\approx 16$\,Hz), a value chosen for stability under the implicit integrator. Simply stiffening the mounts toward the published value does not recover the reference performance: with the authority pole fixed, raising $K_P$ merely injects more force into the flexible modes, so the exposure error remains floored at the scale reported above and degrades once $K_P$ exceeds a few times $10^7$\,N/m, eventually stiffening the equations past what the integrator tolerates. Reaching the reference's nanometre band therefore requires a genuinely higher-bandwidth fine-positioning subsystem --- a higher authority pole with rigid mounts, the 2\,kHz-class stage of the reference machine --- which is left to future work. All subsequent experiments run under Case 3b and reference their thresholds to its own baseline, studying the \textit{relative} effect of trajectory parameters at the error scale this plant provides.

\subsection{The snap bound versus the exposure specification}\label{sec:exp_specsweep}

Having established Case 3b as the reference controller for the full two-stage machine, this subsection settles the question that matters for the rest of the thesis: can a fourth-order trajectory actually \textit{meet} the exposure specification under full closed-loop control, and what does it cost? The snap bound walks the entire wafer across the specification. At the baseline snap ($s = 10^5$), the exposure MSD is 994\,nm and all 49 dies fail, with the classifier assigning the map to \textit{Center}. Tightening the snap ($s = 2\times10^4$, $10^4$, $5\times10^3$) progressively lowers the MSD to 355, 174, and 63.1\,nm. At $s = 10^3$, the MSD reaches 9.5\,nm (2.5\,nm above the 7\,nm bound). The MSD specification is cleared at $s = 100$ (1.0\,nm, 0 of 49 failing, \textit{none}), at the price of extending the scan duration to 785\,ms. The moving-average band $[-1.25, +2]$\,nm remains unreached at the finest tested snap ($s = 50$, $\mathrm{MSD} = 0.5$\,nm, $\mathrm{MA} = 28.7$\,nm, cycle time 55.5\,s), so its crossing point lies below the finest grid point evaluated here.

This is the central mechanism of the thesis, made quantitative: on this two-stage machine, the exposure-window MSD specification is reachable through the \textit{trajectory} alone, by bounding the snap, and throughput is the price. The moving-average band is considerably further away, and its crossing point is not resolved by the present grid. It is not the servo that closes the gap. Raising the actuator-mount stiffness toward the reference machine's rigid value, or the fine loop's bandwidth beyond its $K_P/K_V = 100$\,rad/s pole, both \textit{degrade} the tracking rather than improve it: the residual is the structural ring that the bandwidth-limited loop cannot suppress but that a sufficiently smooth reference never excites. That is why the optimization of Experiment 6 searches the kinematic bounds under the CNN cost --- the specification defines an achievable operating point, and the trajectory that reaches it most cheaply, or that trades a scanner-plausible yield loss for throughput, is what the optimizer must find.

\section{Experiment 5: The Throughput--Yield Transition}\label{sec:exp_transition}

A deterministic trajectory stresses every die almost identically, so sweeping its parameters mainly moves the \textit{count} of failing dies (Experiment 3). Spatially structured, mechanism-bearing patterns can only arise where dies genuinely experience different dynamics. This experiment isolates the strongest such mechanism in the step-and-scan cycle: the settling budget $t_{step}$ between motions. Exposure of each die begins immediately after the preceding step, so any residual transient at scan entry is part of the exposed error; dies whose preceding step is atypical --- above all the row-change dies at the wafer edge, whose repositioning move spans the difference in row length on a circular wafer --- enter the scan with a larger residual. As $t_{step}$ shrinks (raising throughput), those dies should fail first, producing a scanner-plausible edge pattern that emerges from the motion profile itself, with no injected disturbances. Demonstrating this is demonstrating the central premise of the thesis: that trajectory aggressiveness maps to defect morphology, not merely to an error norm.

Two thresholds tell two halves of the story. Against the \textit{absolute} specification --- $\mathrm{MSD} \leq 7$\,nm --- the experiment is quickly settled: with a spec-compliant tight-snap trajectory ($s = 100$, the tightest snap tested in Section~\ref{sec:exp_specsweep} that clears the bound with margin) under Case 3b, shrinking $t_{step}$ from 150 down to 40\,ms leaves \textit{no} die over the bound (0 of 241 at every budget, exposure-window MSD 1.0--1.1\,nm throughout). The fine loop settles the post-step transient well within the exposure window even at 40\,ms, so on this machine the settling budget is not the binding constraint --- the trajectory is. This is the counterpart of Experiment 3: the specification is won or lost on the snap bound, not on the settle time, and the controller absorbs the throughput pressure that a coarser loop would have converted into edge failures.

The spatial signature the central premise predicts nevertheless emerges cleanly when the threshold is referenced to the trajectory's own settled performance rather than to the absolute spec --- the right lens for asking \textit{where} failures first appear, as opposed to \textit{whether} the wafer meets spec. With the baseline trajectory and an onset-referenced threshold (twice the median exposure MSD of the most-settled, 200\,ms run --- 0.79\,\textmu m here), sweeping $t_{step}$ from 200 to 120\,ms tracks two spatial statistics besides the failing count: the \textit{row-change enrichment} (failure rate among post-row-change dies relative to the wafer average) and the \textit{scan-order index} (mean scan position of the failing dies, normalized so 1.0 is uniform). The results (Table~\ref{tab:exp5_transition}, Figure~\ref{fig:exp5_transition_maps}) confirm the mechanism precisely at the transition onset. At $t_{step} = 180$\,ms the first failures appear as a full eight-die cluster with a row-change enrichment of \textbf{15.06}: every one of them is a die scanned immediately after a row change, clustering along one edge of the wafer, visually an \textit{Edge-Loc} morphology in the WM-811K taxonomy and produced purely by the motion profile. The onset is a step rather than a gradual ramp: the cluster is already at its full eight-die extent at the first budget where it appears, and holds at that same size and enrichment down to 160\,ms, still read by the classifier as \textit{none}. The collapse resolves into two distinct stages rather than a single jump: between 160 and 150\,ms the wafer crosses from the eight-die edge cluster to a bulk failure of 103 dies, read as \textit{Loc} rather than \textit{Center} --- the classifier's closest available reading for a large but not yet wafer-wide failure population, given its zero \textit{Scratch} recall (Experiment 2) --- and only between 150 and 120\,ms does the map saturate into the \textit{Center}-classified blob, with 232 of 241 dies failing. The wafer cycle time falls from 82.1 to 62.8\,s across the sweep, making the throughput--yield trade explicit: the settling budget between the clean 190\,ms wafer and the onset at 180\,ms buys nothing, while pushing from 160 to 150\,ms trades a modest cycle-time gain for the bulk of the wafer.

Two observations qualify the classifier's part. The onset cluster is below the CNN's sensitivity --- it still reports \textit{none} at 96--97\,\%, and the cost even \textit{dips} slightly once the cluster appears at its eight-die extent (from 0.041 on the clean wafer to 0.037): a reminder that the cost is a probability of morphology, not a die count, and the seed of the calibration behaviour Experiment 6 exposes. The bulk failure at 150\,ms is read as \textit{Loc} rather than \textit{Center}, so the corrected cost~\eqref{eq:cost_corrected} still credits it as a scanner-plausible (if severe) pattern; only once the wafer fully saturates at 120\,ms does the classifier leave the plausible subset, reading the map as \textit{Center} --- a class the cost prices at its maximum as an out-of-domain signal rather than at zero. The informative segment of the cost curve --- exactly as anticipated --- is the transition region, and it is there that the optimizer of Experiment 6 operates. The transition thus carries two readings: against the real specification the controller absorbs the settling budget --- snap, not settle time, is the lever (Experiment 3) --- while against an onset-referenced threshold the first trajectory-induced failures are unambiguously the row-change dies, a scanner-plausible \textit{Edge-Loc} edge cluster produced by the motion profile alone.

\section{Experiment 6: Optimization Scenarios E3 and E4}\label{sec:exp_pso}

The final experiment runs the particle swarm of Section~\ref{sec:methodology_pso} twice over the same plant, controller (Case 3b), and settling budget ($t_{step} = 150$\,ms), with every die judged against the absolute exposure-window specification $\mathrm{MSD} \leq 7$\,nm of Eq.~\eqref{eq:ma_msd_spec}, changing only the cost. Both scenarios carry a common throughput reward $\gamma\, T_{wafer}/T_{ref}$ ($\gamma = 1$, $T_{ref} = 15$\,s), so that a faster wafer is cheaper and the search actively trades cycle time against the map cost: scenario E3 pairs it with the conventional servo cost (wafer-average exposure MSD), scenario E4 with the yield-aware CNN cost~\eqref{eq:cost_corrected}. The question is what each cost is willing to trade throughput \textit{for}. The search runs over the exact fifteen-segment profile family, so all four bounds --- including $s_{\max}$ --- are live dimensions. Six particles run for eight iterations (48 full-wafer simulations per scenario), with one particle seeded at the baseline trajectory.

Against the absolute 7\,nm specification the two baselines fail outright (Table~\ref{tab:exp6_pso}): the fourth-order baseline saturates the wafer --- 49 of 49 dies over the bound, classified \textit{Center} --- and the third-order baseline is worse still. The servo-cost search (E3) converges cleanly on a \textit{spec-compliant} trajectory: six particles over eight iterations (the standard, unseeded configuration of Section~\ref{sec:methodology_pso}) find a clean wafer (0 of 49 failing, $\mathcal{J}_{map} = 0.043$) at $v \approx 0.67$\,m/s, $a \approx 44.9$\,m/s$^2$, $j = 5000$\,m/s$^3$ --- acceleration and jerk pinned at the top of their search range --- with the snap tightened to $s \approx 170$\,m/s$^4$, near the bottom of the search range, confirming that the snap bound, not velocity or acceleration, is what the exposure error is most sensitive to: once $s$ is small enough, the search is free to run acceleration and jerk aggressively ($v \approx 0.67$\,m/s, $a \approx 44.9$\,m/s$^2$, $j = 5000$\,m/s$^3$, cycle time 37.6\,s).

The CNN-cost search (E4) exposes a real difference between the two costs as \textit{optimization landscapes}, not only as evaluation criteria. With the same standard configuration, E4 never leaves the \textit{Center}-saturation plateau in 48 evaluations for \textit{either} of two seeds tried: every candidate either swarm visits fails 49 of 49 dies, the best found being $\mathcal{J}_{map} = 2.000$ at $v \approx 0.82$\,m/s (default seed) and at $v \approx 0.77$\,m/s, $a \approx 34.8$\,m/s$^2$ (an alternate seed), and because the corrected cost~\eqref{eq:cost_corrected} prices every such map at the same $\mathcal{J}_{map} = 2.000$ ceiling regardless of \textit{how} saturated it is, neither swarm receives a gradient to climb out --- unlike the servo cost, whose $\mathrm{mean}(\mathrm{MSD})$ term is continuous and strictly decreasing as the trajectory gentles, even while every die still fails. Because the compliant snap region (Section~\ref{sec:exp_specsweep}) occupies a small fraction of the wide search box, 48 evaluations from either unseeded particle swarm are not enough to land inside it by chance. This is not a contradiction of hypothesis H3: E3, searching the identical widened box under the servo cost, still finds the acceptable region reliably (previous paragraph) --- the acceptable-pattern region demonstrably exists, and the CNN cost would correctly price a candidate there below the saturated baselines if the swarm ever reached one. What the wider box and two stuck seeds show more starkly than before is that the yield-aware cost's flatness on the far side of the specification --- the same property that, on the near side, is deliberately exploited to let the optimizer spend an acceptable-pattern yield loss for throughput (Sections~\ref{sec:exp_specsweep} and \ref{sec:exp_transition}) --- is a liability for gradient-free search whenever the swarm starts, or is trapped, entirely within the saturated region: there is nothing in the cost to tell it which direction is better, and a proportionally smaller target region only makes an undirected search less likely to stumble into it. The servo cost has no such blind spot, which is exactly why E3 converges reliably from the same seeded start on the same box. The practical remedy is the one this result reinforces rather than merely suggests: seed additional particles away from the saturated baseline (the present PSO seeds only one, at the baseline itself), or hybridize the search by warm-starting the CNN-cost swarm from the servo-cost optimum, so that gradient information from the smoother cost bootstraps the pattern-aware one into its own acceptable region before evaluating it there. That regime --- calibrating the CNN cost's usable gradient, and the classifier behavior a coarser specification would expose --- is the natural continuation of this work. The cost-evaluation history of all runs is archived with the experiment scripts.

\section{Experiment 7: Does Trajectory Smoothness Matter Without a Good Controller?}\label{sec:exp_order}

The premise of higher-order trajectory design is that bounding deeper derivatives injects less energy into the structure. A fair question is whether that refinement survives contact with a mediocre controller: if the tracking error is dominated by servo lag, does smoothing the reference accomplish anything at all? This experiment crosses the profile order --- acceleration-bounded (second order), jerk-bounded (third), and snap-bounded (fourth, exact fifteen-segment generator, plus a tight-snap variant at $s_{\max} = 2\times10^4$\,m/s$^4$) --- with two representative control setups: bare collocated PID (Case 1) and feedforward plus fine-stage HIGS (Case 3b). All profiles run at the same kinematic bounds $(0.8, 20, 1600)$ on the four-die row.

The answer is layered (Table~\ref{tab:exp7_order}, Figure~\ref{fig:exp7_order}). \textbf{Under the bare PID, trajectory order reaches the exposure only through the transients.} The cruise MA sits at 0.7--0.95\,mm for every order --- it is the slowly decaying tail of the acceleration transient, which the collocated loop cannot reject, and reference smoothness does not remove it within the cruise window. Order does shape how much transient there is to decay: the cruise MSD improves monotonically with smoothness, $53.0 \rightarrow 34.5 \rightarrow 25.0 \rightarrow 11.9$\,\textmu m from second order to tight snap ($4.4\times$), and the full-scan MSD follows the same ordering ($278 \rightarrow 64$\,\textmu m); the reduction is real but the exposure error remains dominated by what the controller leaves behind.

\textbf{Under full control (Case 3b), trajectory order is still worth roughly a factor of thirty.} With the feedforward removing the quasi-static lag and the HIGS loop suppressing what lies within its bandwidth, the cruise MSD falls $24.0 \rightarrow 3.27 \rightarrow 0.834$\,\textmu m from second to fourth order --- a $\approx29\times$ improvement attributable to the profile alone, at identical kinematic bounds, and $\approx77\times$ to the tight-snap variant (312\,nm). The interpretation is classical: the fine loop handles the error components inside its bandwidth, and the profile order determines how much excitation exists \textit{beyond} it; smoothing the reference and improving the controller are complements, not substitutes. Two further readings matter for the optimizer. First, smoothness has a throughput price --- $T_{scan}$ grows 21\,\% from second to fourth order at the same bounds, and a further 30\,\% for the tight-snap variant --- so profile order participates in the same throughput--yield trade as the kinematic bounds themselves. Second, the tight-snap variant remains the \textit{most} accurate configuration in cruise (MSD 312\,nm, MA 2.32\,\textmu m), yet its full-scan MSD is no better than the plain fourth-order profile's (4.96 vs.\ 4.59\,\textmu m, both set by the post-step entry transient) while spending 30\,\% more scan time. Accuracy inside the cruise keeps improving with smoothness, while the settling-dominated component saturates at the closed loop's floor and the throughput cost grows without bound: the optimum in the snap dimension is therefore interior once throughput enters the cost --- precisely the trade the optimizer of Experiment 6 is built to resolve.


\section{Discussion}\label{sec:exp_discussion}

Read against the evaluation scenarios of the thesis proposal --- E1: third-order baseline, E2: fourth-order baseline, E3: optimization under a servo cost, E4: optimization under the CNN cost --- the experiments above address all four: the trajectory generator, the validated plant, the progressive control configurations up to the nonlinear HIGS loop, the trained classifier, the trajectory-to-map bridge, and two full optimization runs differing only in their cost. The third-versus-fourth-order comparison of E1 and E2 is delivered directly by the order study of Experiment 7, now that the exact segment generators represent every profile order by its true shape rather than by a polynomial approximation.

Of the validation criterion, three elements are now satisfied: unperturbed operation maps to the \textit{none} class; the cost input shifts monotonically and reproducibly under parameter sweeps; and --- the strongest result of the campaign --- spatially structured, scanner-plausible morphology emerges from the motion profile itself, with the transition onset of Experiment 5 concentrating its first failures at the row-change dies with fifteen-fold enrichment, an \textit{Edge-Loc} pattern requiring no injected disturbance.

The class-level half of the criterion --- that motion-induced failures be \textit{classified} into the scanner-plausible classes --- is met only partially, and every failure mode observed points at the classifier rather than at the simulation half of the pipeline. The scratch-shaped disturbance cannot be recognized as \textit{Scratch} while the classifier's recall for that class is zero (Experiment 2), so the 15-die column falls to \textit{Loc}; dense scattered patterns are absorbed into \textit{Center}; the drift annulus is read as \textit{Donut}; the Edge-Loc onset of Experiment 5 sits below the classifier's sensitivity; and the classifier prices \textit{morphology}, not die count, so on the near-clean plateau it reads every sparse-failure wafer as \textit{none}. Experiment 6 turned that last property to advantage --- it is exactly what lets the yield-aware cost trade an acceptable-pattern failure for throughput --- but it also means the cost cannot, on its own, resolve fine yield differences the classifier was never trained to see. The refinement list is correspondingly concrete and bounded: rebalanced training for the minority classes (above all \textit{Scratch}) and probability calibration on clean and near-clean maps, so that motion-induced scratch and edge morphologies are recognized as themselves rather than as their nearest well-populated neighbour. On the trajectory side the exact generators are now in place, and Experiment 7 showed that profile order is worth an order of magnitude of exposure error under a capable controller, with the snap trade-off resolved by throughput rather than by accuracy alone. On the plant side, error-velocity servo damping on the frictionless guideways and balance-mass reaction cancellation hold the exposure error at the sub-micrometre scale under full control (Case 3b); the remaining gap to the reference study's tens-of-nanometre result reflects the fine-stage servo bandwidth ($100$\,rad/s authority pole modeled here vs.\ high-bandwidth positioning subsystems). None of these items changes the architecture validated here: generate, track, map, classify, price, and optimize --- the loop is closed and runs end to end.
